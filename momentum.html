Cross-Sectional Momentum in U.S. Large Caps

Shaotong (Tom) Li  ·   · 

Abstract

This paper tests whether a stock's past 12-month return, excluding the most recent month, predicts its cross-sectional return over the following month within a point-in-time S&P 500 universe. Ranking within each sector into quintiles by a walk-forward composite of momentum (12-1, 6-1, 3-1) and information discreteness, whose factor weights are re-learned monthly out-of-sample, the top quintile earns a gross Sharpe of 0.95, and a market-neutral long-short (top minus bottom quintile) earns a modest, cost-sensitive premium, while the composite's linear cross-sectional signal is positive but weak. Because the universe is survivorship-free (delisted names retained), these estimates are deliberately conservative; results are reported gross and net of a simple transaction-cost model, with limitations stated plainly.

Headline results

1. Data and signal construction

The universe is point-in-time: S&P 500 membership is reconstructed month by month from historical constituent lists, and delisted names are retained with prices back-filled from a separate vendor, so the backtest carries no survivorship bias. Prices are cleaned for OHLC consistency, non-positive values, and zero-volume flats; all signals and forward returns are shifted strictly within each ticker to preclude look-ahead.

A composite of four factors: three momentum legs (12-1, 6-1, 3-1) and an information-discreteness factor (the "frog-in-the-pan" measure, favouring momentum built from smooth, continuous moves rather than a few large jumps). Rather than fixed weights, the blend is re-estimated every month, out-of-sample: each factor's weight is its trailing-36-month clustered-OLS t-statistic (kept when positive, otherwise dropped), then normalized. Each factor is cross-sectionally z-scored (winsorized 1%/99%); the composite is ranked into quintiles within its sector.

3. Limitations

The composite's cross-sectional signal is positive but weak: its univariate regression t-statistic of 0.8 falls to 0.3 once standard errors are clustered by date, below conventional significance. The lower quintiles are non-monotone, so the edge is concentrated in the top bucket. Survivorship bias is already addressed by the point-in-time universe; three caveats remain:

  • Close-to-close returns. No open/VWAP execution modeling; realized fills would differ.
  • Constant linear costs. Ten basis points per side omits market impact, borrow, and spread dynamics.
  • Short sample, one regime. Roughly nine years spanning a single, technology-led bull market; the estimates need not generalize to other environments.
